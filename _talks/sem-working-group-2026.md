## Abstract

The Hamas attack on Israel on October 7, 2023, sparked renewed public debate in Germany about how to distinguish antisemitism from (non-antisemitic) criticism of Israeli politics. This study empirically examines whether such a distinction still holds and whether there are differences between different political orientations. Using survey data from 2024 (n = 2,082, quota sample of the adult German population), we distinguish four constructs: classical antisemitism, secondary antisemitism, Israel-related antisemitism, and (non-antisemitic) criticism of Israeli politics. Confirmatory factor analyses show that criticism of Israel is empirically distinct from antisemitism, with substantially lower correlations compared to those between the antisemitism dimensions themselves. These distinctions are consistent across political orientations and religious affiliations. Our findings reveal that authoritarianism, conspiracy mentality, and religious fundamentalism strongly predict antisemitic attitudes—especially classical and Israel-related forms—but do not explain criticism of Israeli policies. Political orientation plays a key role: right-leaning individuals show higher levels of antisemitism and tend to blur the line between criticism and antisemitism. Left-leaning individuals express more criticism of Israeli policies but tend to reject antisemitic views.
